





Introduction:


The conduct of free and fair elections is an essential expression of the constitutional principle of adult suffrage under Article 326 of the Constitution of India. The Election Commission of India (ECI), as the constitutional authority entrusted with the superintendence, direction and control of elections under Article 324, has progressively adopted information and communication technologies to improve electoral administration and facilitate the exercise of the franchise.
At present, an elector arriving at a polling station undergoes a series of identification and procedural checks. According to the ECI's current voter guidance, the first polling official checks the elector's name in the electoral roll and verifies the identity document; thereafter, the second polling official performs further prescribed procedures including application of indelible ink, issuance of the voter's slip and obtaining the elector's signature in Form 17A.
The time required for voter check-in is therefore an important operational factor in determining polling-station capacity and queue formation. International election-administration research demonstrates that the check-in stage can become a significant bottleneck. A Massachusetts Institute of Technology/Bipartisan Policy Center study of 3,119 polling places in the United States recorded a mean voter waiting time of 8.9 minutes and a median of 4.7 minutes. The same study demonstrated that, for a hypothetical polling station with one check-in station capable of processing one voter per minute, an arrival rate of 40 voters per hour produced approximately two minutes of average waiting time, while 55 voters per hour produced approximately eleven minutes and 59 voters per hour produced nearly an hour of waiting.
Research on electronic poll books provides further evidence that voter identification and check-in can be substantially streamlined through digital systems. A certified electronic poll-book system evaluated in California reported processing approximately 35–40 seconds per voter. Other election-system research, however, demonstrates that merely replacing paper with electronic systems does not automatically reduce overall waiting time; system design, staffing, voter arrival patterns and polling-place procedures remain important factors.
In India, the ECI has already introduced technology directed towards this particular problem. The ECI's Booth App facilitates faster identification of electors through encrypted QR codes associated with the digital marked copy of the electoral roll, with the stated objectives of faster elector identification, reduced queue load and faster polling. The ECI's Voter Helpline and Voters' Services Portal also enable electors to search the electoral roll, verify their registration status and obtain polling-station information before election day.
The proposed system builds upon this direction by moving a substantial portion of identity and record verification from the polling station to the pre-election period.




Review of Literature:


Existing electoral technology can broadly be divided into four relevant categories.
1. Electoral-roll and QR-based verification in India
The ECI Booth App uses an encrypted QR code for rapid identification of electors from the digital marked copy of the electoral roll. The ECI states that this facilitates fast elector search, instant identification, reduction of queue load and faster polling.
The existing ECI Voter Helpline and Voters' Services Portal allow electors to search their electoral-roll records before election day and obtain information relating to registration and polling stations.
These systems demonstrate that electoral-record verification can already be partially shifted from polling day to the pre-election period.
2. Electronic Poll Books
Electronic Poll Books (EPBs), used extensively in several jurisdictions in the United States, replace or supplement paper-based voter registers for polling-station check-in. The U.S. Election Assistance Commission reported that 41 states and territories used electronic poll books in the 2024 general election, while 40% of jurisdictions reported using them for voter check-in. The EAC identifies faster voter check-in, improved accuracy and real-time voter-registration information as important advantages.
Research and operational assessments indicate that electronic systems can process individual voters considerably faster than traditional paper-based processes in suitable environments. One certified electronic poll-book system reports approximately 35–40 seconds per voter.
However, academic research also cautions that electronic poll books alone do not guarantee shorter overall queues. A 2022 study found that after accounting for polling-place characteristics and poll-worker demographics, electronic poll books had no statistically significant effect on overall voter waiting time. This indicates that a successful solution must address the complete verification workflow rather than merely digitise the existing paper process.
3. Biometric verification
Countries such as Brazil and Ghana have employed biometric voter-identification mechanisms to establish the identity of electors and reduce the possibility of impersonation and duplicate voting. Such systems demonstrate the potential of combining an electoral record with an additional identity factor.
However, biometric verification introduces additional considerations relating to privacy, false matches, accessibility, inclusion, data security and legal authority. Consequently, the proposed system treats face verification and liveness detection as supporting verification mechanisms rather than as an independent authority for determining electoral eligibility.
4. Government-issued digital credentials
India already possesses digital mechanisms capable of supporting document verification. UIDAI's Aadhaar Secure QR is digitally signed and can be used for offline identity verification. UIDAI states that its Secure QR contains demographic information and photograph and that its authenticity can be validated through the prescribed verification mechanism.
Similarly, DigiLocker provides access to documents issued directly by authorised issuers and states that issued documents are legally at par with physical originals. Its architecture supports consent-based sharing and verification of documents directly obtained from issuing authorities.
These technologies provide a foundation for a system in which a citizen presents a trusted, issuer-backed credential rather than uploading an unverified photograph or scanned document.

Rationale of Study:


The central problem addressed by the study is not the absence of voter records, but the time and administrative effort required to establish and verify an elector's identity at the appropriate stage of the electoral process.
The existing ECI ecosystem already permits citizens to search their electoral records before election day. Nevertheless, verification-related activities may still need to be performed at the polling station.
International polling-place research demonstrates that check-in capacity has a direct relationship with queue formation. In one MIT/BPC model, a single check-in station capable of processing one voter per minute could accommodate 40 arrivals per hour with approximately two minutes of average waiting time; at 55 arrivals per hour, average waiting increased to approximately eleven minutes.
Other research has recorded an average check-in processing time of approximately 1.63 minutes in observed election data, while a Georgia Tech election-resource model uses two minutes as a representative check-in mean for simulation. A California electronic poll-book assessment reported approximately 35–40 seconds per voter.
These figures should not be interpreted as an Indian national average. Rather, they establish a credible international benchmark showing that verification/check-in is measurable, variable and capable of becoming a queue-forming bottleneck.
The proposed study therefore seeks to measure the corresponding Indian baseline rather than assume it.
For example, if a controlled field study finds that the current verification-related interaction takes an average of 90 seconds per elector and the proposed pre-verification workflow reduces the polling-station interaction to 40 seconds, the direct saving would be approximately 50 seconds per elector. At 800 electors, this would represent approximately 400 minutes, or 6.67 hours, of cumulative polling-staff processing time. Such figures would be reported only after empirical measurement and would not be assumed beforehand.
The rationale is consequently to shift verification work from the high-pressure polling-day environment to a controlled pre-election environment, while preserving the statutory role of election officials in final electoral verification.








Objective of Study:


The primary objective is to design and evaluate a pre-election online verification platform capable of reducing verification-related workload at polling stations while maintaining the principles of accuracy, inclusiveness, security, privacy and human oversight.
The specific objectives are:
To provide electors with a mechanism for verifying their electoral records before election day.
To reduce the amount of electoral-record searching and identity-document examination required at the polling station.
To support rapid retrieval of an elector's record using EPIC number and other legally permissible electoral-record search mechanisms.
To permit verification through trusted, issuer-backed identity credentials where legally and technically permissible.
To distinguish between:
electoral-record verification,
identity-document verification,
face detection,
face matching, and
liveness detection.
To identify inconsistencies without automatically denying an elector's right to participate merely because of an automated mismatch.
To route uncertain cases to a designated human verification process, particularly the appropriate Booth Level Officer or other authorised election official.
To measure the difference between the existing/manual verification workflow and the proposed pre-verification workflow in terms of:
average processing time,
median processing time,
95th-percentile processing time,
number of manual interventions,
number of unresolved cases,
queue length,
verification success rate, and
polling-staff workload.
To evaluate whether pre-election verification can reduce peak polling-station congestion.
To develop a prototype architecture that can subsequently be evaluated by competent electoral authorities for legal, security and operational feasibility.








Methodology:


The proposed system will operate as a pre-election verification and assistance platform, rather than as an independent authority determining an elector's eligibility.
Stage 1: Electoral-record verification
The elector first attempts to identify the relevant electoral record through the authorised ECI mechanism.
Three possible user routes are proposed:
A. EPIC number
This is the preferred route where the elector possesses the EPIC number.
B. Search by personal details
This route is intended for electors who do not have their EPIC number readily available and is subject to the fields and search facilities legally and technically made available by the competent electoral authority.
C. Registered mobile number
This route may be implemented only where the relevant official electoral service supports such a lookup.
If a matching electoral record is found, the system displays the retrieved information and asks the elector to confirm that the record corresponds to them.
If no appropriate record is identified, the system does not declare the person ineligible. Instead, it generates a verification exception and directs the case towards the prescribed manual/BLO assistance process.
Stage 2: Supporting identity credential
After identifying the electoral record, the system may request one additional trusted identity credential.
Possible categories for the prototype are:
Aadhaar Secure QR / legally permissible Aadhaar verification mechanism
DigiLocker-issued document
PAN
Driving Licence
Other government-issued credential subsequently authorised for the purpose
The proposed system will prefer issuer-backed or digitally verifiable information over user-uploaded images.
For example, UIDAI's Secure QR is digitally signed and specifically designed for offline identity verification. DigiLocker provides issuer-backed documents and supports consent-based verification.
For the academic prototype, actual government integrations shall be represented through mock or sandbox interfaces unless formal access and authorisation are obtained.
Stage 3: Information comparison
The supporting identity information is compared with the electoral record.
Potential comparison fields include:
Name
Date of birth, where available
Age
Photograph
Gender, where legally relevant
Other permitted identifying attributes
The result is categorised as:
Consistent
The available identity information is sufficiently consistent and the process proceeds to the next stage.
Minor discrepancy
The discrepancy is recorded and may proceed according to predefined rules or require human confirmation.
Significant discrepancy
The system does not automatically reject the elector. The case is marked for BLO/manual review.
This approach is essential because automated matching should assist election officials rather than replace statutory human decision-making.
Stage 4: Face detection
The camera system first determines whether a human face is present.
Face detection is technically distinct from face verification.
Stage 5: Face verification
The live facial image is compared against an authorised photograph associated with the verified identity credential or electoral record, subject to the legal and technical permissions applicable to the system.
The system produces a confidence/result classification rather than an independent legal determination.
Stage 6: Liveness detection
The system verifies that the presented subject is a live person rather than a photograph, screen reproduction or other presentation attack.
For the prototype, the system may use randomized challenges such as:
Looking left
Looking right
Blinking
Looking towards the camera
The challenge sequence should be randomized to prevent a fixed interaction pattern.
A failed liveness test results in manual/BLO review rather than automatic rejection.
Stage 7: Final verification classification
The proposed system generates one of two principal outcomes:
Verification Successful
Electoral record identified
Identity information sufficiently consistent
Face verification successful, where applicable
Liveness successful
Manual/BLO Review Required
Electoral record not identified
Significant identity discrepancy
Face verification failure
Liveness failure
Technical failure
Insufficient or conflicting information
The system will therefore follow:
ECI Electoral Record → EPIC / Permitted Details Lookup → Record Confirmation → Supporting Credential → Information Comparison → Face Verification → Liveness Challenge → Verification Successful / BLO Review

Result:

The expected result is a functional prototype demonstrating that a significant portion of verification-related activity can be completed before election day, thereby reducing the amount of searching, document examination and identity comparison required at the polling station.
The study is expected to establish an empirically measured value for the time saved per elector rather than assuming a predetermined saving.
Existing research provides useful benchmarks against which the prototype may be evaluated. Published election-administration research has reported check-in processing times ranging from approximately 35–40 seconds in an electronic poll-book implementation to approximately 1.63–2 minutes in other observed or modelled polling environments.
Accordingly, the project may use the following as an evaluation hypothesis, not as a claimed Indian result:
If pre-election verification substantially resolves the elector's identity and electoral-record questions before arrival, the polling-station verification interaction should approach a short confirmation process rather than a complete record-search and identity-verification process.
For example, a hypothetical reduction from 90 seconds to 40 seconds would save 50 seconds per elector. For 800 electors, this would amount to approximately 400 minutes of cumulative processing time. The actual value will be determined through controlled testing.
The system is also expected to produce a structured verification record identifying the reason for successful verification or referral to BLO/manual review.

Discussion:

The principal contribution of the proposed system is not merely the digitisation of the electoral roll. The ECI has already undertaken substantial digital transformation through systems such as the Voter Helpline, Voters' Services Portal and Booth App.
The proposed study instead examines whether the verification burden can be shifted temporally: from election day, when polling officials operate under strict procedural and time constraints, to the pre-election period, when the elector can voluntarily verify their information in advance.
This approach has several potential advantages.
First, the polling official may receive a structured verification result instead of having to perform every preliminary identity check from the beginning.
Second, discrepancies can be identified before election day, allowing the elector to seek appropriate assistance rather than discovering the issue at the polling station.
Third, trusted digital credentials can reduce dependence on manually examining photographs or documents. DigiLocker, for example, is expressly designed to provide issuer-backed documents and verification with citizen consent.
Fourth, the system can provide quantitative evidence regarding polling-station workload. Rather than claiming that technology will automatically reduce queues, the project will measure the actual effect.
This distinction is important. Research has shown that electronic poll books alone do not necessarily reduce overall voter waiting time. Queue formation depends on arrival rates, processing capacity, staffing and the complete sequence of activities at the polling place. Consequently, the proposed system should be evaluated as a process optimisation intervention, not simply as a software replacement for a paper register.
The proposed architecture also deliberately retains human oversight. An automated mismatch should not, by itself, deprive a citizen of the opportunity to vote. The system should identify discrepancies and provide evidence to the competent election official, while the final decision remains within the authority and procedure prescribed by law.
Privacy and constitutional considerations are equally important. The system should follow the principles of purpose limitation, data minimisation, consent where applicable, access control, encryption, auditability and limited retention. Aadhaar-related processing, in particular, must comply with the applicable statutory and regulatory framework; UIDAI's own offline-verification mechanisms demonstrate that verification can be performed without unnecessarily retaining the Aadhaar number.
The system should also avoid creating a new barrier to the exercise of the franchise. An elector who does not possess a smartphone, cannot complete online verification, has a disability, has poor connectivity, or encounters a technical failure must continue to have access to the prescribed election-day verification procedure. Pre-election verification should therefore be an assistance mechanism and not a condition for exercising the right to vote.
The final objective is consequently not to replace the constitutional and statutory election machinery with an artificial-intelligence system. It is to use technology to make that machinery more efficient, transparent and citizen-centric while preserving the authority of election officials.
The proposed system can therefore be viewed as a pre-election digital verification layer supporting the existing electoral administration framework, with the potential to reduce polling-day verification workload, identify discrepancies earlier, shorten verification interactions and provide measurable operational improvements without transferring the final electoral decision from authorised officials to an automated system.
